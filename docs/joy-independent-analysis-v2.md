### Joy's share: our arithmetic

The 2019 Bond Bank credit review said Joy was $1.1 million of a $6,950,000 tax-exempt request. The bonds actually sold were $5,765,000 par, producing a $6,813,000 loan with premium. Pro rata:

- By loan proceeds: 1,100,000 / 6,813,000 × 5,765,000 = **about $931,000** of par.
- By the application figures: 1,100,000 / 6,950,000 × 5,765,000 = **about $912,000** of par.

Call it **$0.9 million of Series U par allocable to Joy**, all of it still outstanding, all of it callable on 1 May 2029. The Borough's bond compliance file holds the actual allocation of proceeds, and only that file can confirm the number. This is arithmetic, not an audit.

### What that means for a transaction before 2029

Treasury Regulation 1.141-12 lets an issuer cure a deliberate action by redeeming the nonqualified bonds at the earliest call date, or, if the bonds cannot be redeemed within 90 days, by establishing a defeasance escrow within 90 days. Since the call date is 1 May 2029, a sale or private lease in 2027 would mean an escrow sufficient to pay interest at 5 percent to the call date and principal on it. On $0.93 million that is on the order of **$1.0 to $1.05 million gross, before escrow earnings**, for a closing in early 2027, and less each month after that as the call date approaches.

Three things bond counsel decides that could make the number much smaller, or zero:

1. **Which issue is measured.** The private business use test is a 10 percent test. Joy is about 16 percent of the Borough's tax-exempt loan but under 4 percent of the Bond Bank's $28,445,000 2019 Series One issue. Whether the test runs at the loan or the pooled issue level is a technical question with a large effect on the answer.
2. **Alternative use of disposition proceeds.** If the Borough sells for cash and reasonably expects to spend the proceeds within two years on other qualifying capital projects, that is a remedy that costs the Borough no cash at all.
3. **How much of the building.** Only the portion put to private business use counts. A partial lease, or a lease to a governmental user, changes the fraction.

### One point that cuts the other way

Treasury Regulation 1.141-12(a)(3) conditions remedial action on the arrangement being bona fide and arm's-length, with the new user paying **fair market value** for the use of the financed property. That reads against a below-fair-rental-value lease of the bond-financed portion under FNSBC 20.16.020(D), because a $1-a-year lease to a nonprofit is private business use with no fair market value consideration. The Newby Park precedent in Ordinance 2025-15 involved no bond-financed property.

The same paragraph adds a door: fair market value may take into account governmental use restrictions placed on the property. A lease that carries real public-use obligations, priced against those obligations, may still be fair market value under the regulation even if it is below fair rental value under Borough Code. Whether that door is open for Joy is a question for bond counsel, not a conclusion, but it is the kind of question that should be asked before a lease structure is drafted, not after.

## 2. Older debt: closed

The Borough's Index of Propositions, 1963 to current, records **Ordinance No. 2000-012 as FAILED** at a 2000 special election, 5,241 yes to 6,098 no. It was a $57,000,000 bond for upgrades at Hunter, Hutchison, North Pole Elementary, North Pole High, North Pole Middle, Two Rivers, Denali and Nordale, with playgrounds and technology upgrades elsewhere. Joy appeared only under "technology infrastructure upgrades at Weller, Woodriver, Pearl Creek, Badger, Two Rivers, Joy and U-Park Elementary Schools."

No bonds were issued under it. No other proposition in the index names Joy. The Borough's other outstanding school bonds, 2016 Series T and 2020 Series W, trace to the 2011 and 2013 authorizations (Ryan, Salcha, Woodriver, North Pole Middle and High, Ticasuk Brown, University Park, Tanana, Two Rivers) and to refundings of 2006 to 2012 series. None of those names Joy.

**The 2019 Series U is the only bond debt on the building, and it carries no State reimbursement condition.**

## 4. The lease precedent, verbatim

Ordinance No. 2025-15, adopted 24 July 2025, nine to zero. Sponsors: Wilson, Haney, Crass, Rotermund, LaJiness. Three weeks after Joy closed.

**Title.** "An ordinance authorizing the Mayor to enter into a lease agreement at less than fair rental value with the Friends of the North Pole Park and Recreation Complex on Borough land identified as Tract B-1-A, Conby Acres Subdivision, known as Newby Park ... and waiving the requirements of FNSBC 20.16.020(C) related to the competitive process requirement."

**Terms.** One dollar a year. Initial term four years, two renewal options of four years each. Lessee carries all maintenance and utilities. Lessee must ensure free public use and rental opportunities for other recreational nonprofits.

**The finding, Section 2, verbatim.** "The Assembly hereby finds that granting this lease at less than fair rental value meets the conditions of a compelling public purpose based upon the following facts:

1. Friends of the North Pole Park and Recreation Complex's mission is to instill in the Nation's youth a sincere desire to develop within themselves a feeling of citizenship, sportsmanship, loyalty and team spirit, and to aid in the improvement and development of the physical fitness of our country's youth.
2. Friends of the North Pole Park and Recreation Complex is a non-profit entity that has a network of volunteers to maintain the park and maximize recreational opportunities for the North Pole area and will provide the community at-large the ability to utilize the park.
3. The lease will allow the rehabilitation and improvement of a degrading facility."

**The waiver rationale, from the recitals.** "A competitive process was conducted for a smaller portion of the parcel and did not get any responses; further, the property has been used as a park, is zoned Outdoor Recreational District, and there is a user group that is ready, willing, and able to operate the facility for recreational uses."

**What this establishes.** The standard the Assembly actually applied is three short factual findings: a mission, a nonprofit with capacity, and rehabilitation of a degrading facility. The waiver of the competitive process rested on a prior competitive attempt with no responses. For Joy, a competitive attempt has not yet been made, so the waiver rationale used at Newby Park is not yet available.

## 5. The procurement clock

FNSBC 20.16.020(C) sends a competitive lease of Borough land to Title 16. Title 16 is the procurement code; its relevant section is **16.12.020, Competitive sealed proposals**:

- Used when the Chief Procurement Officer determines in writing that sealed bidding is not practicable or advantageous.
- Solicited through a request for proposals with public notice of **not less than 30 calendar days**.
- The RFP must state the relative importance of price and other evaluation factors. No other factors may be used.
- Discussions and best-and-final offers are permitted.
- Award to the offeror whose proposal is "most advantageous to the Borough," with the basis recorded in the contract file.

**16.12.090** requires Assembly or Finance Committee approval for any contract over $200,000. **20.16.020(B)** requires an ordinance for any lease regardless.

**Timeline implication.** From RFP issue: 30 days minimum notice, then evaluation and any best-and-final round, then a lease ordinance with introduction, advancement and public hearing across two Assembly meetings. Three to four months from RFP to authorization is a realistic floor. The request for information that the mayor has said precedes the RFP has no clock in code at all. None of this begins until the appraisal is in hand, on the current sequencing.

## 6. The carrying cost, sharpened

Two public figures from June 2025 that version 1 did not have:

- The ordinance to keep Joy operating through 30 June 2026 was reported at **$69,590** for the fiscal year (Fairbanks Daily News-Miner, 16 June 2025). The Assembly rejected it five to four. The building has since cost roughly **$140,000 a year** to keep closed, on the mayor's own figure. If the reported figure holds, closing cost about twice what keeping it open would have. **Caution:** the two numbers measure different things, a community centre operating budget and an empty building's utilities, and the $69,590 comes from a single news report. Read the June 2025 ordinance and its fiscal note before leaning on the comparison.
- The same reporting put the deferred repair need at **$3 million for HVAC and $2 million for roof**. The roof figure needs reconciling with the 2019 bond, which paid $1.1 million to replace the roof, insulation and clerestories six years earlier. Either the 2019 scope was partial, or the $2 million is an estimate for something else. Only the Borough's project file answers that.

The FY 2026-2027 budget records the closure in two lines: Joy Community Center public service hours falling from 1,331 to zero, and "Relocated staff from Joy Community Center to other facilities." It does not itemize the building's utilities; they sit inside a facilities line.
