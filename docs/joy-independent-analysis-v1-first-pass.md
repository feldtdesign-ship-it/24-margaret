# Joy: Independent Analysis

**A public-records answer to the five questions in the September 12, 2026 letter to Assemblymember Wilson.**

Compiled: September 12, 2026
Source basis: public records only. No privileged material, no Borough staff time, no FOIA request.
Status: first pass. Version 1.

---

## Why this document exists

Dan Keck's letter of September 12, 2026 asked the Fairbanks North Star Borough for a written report on five areas concerning the former Joy Elementary School / Joy Community Center.

This document is our own attempt at those same five areas, assembled from publicly available records on the day the letter went out. It is offered in the spirit the letter describes: we are not asking the Borough to skip analysis, we are demonstrating that a meaningful portion of that analysis is already sitting in public documents and can be assembled quickly.

Where we could not determine something from public records, we say so plainly and identify who holds the answer. Those gaps are the real work, and they are short.

## 2. Financial and legal obligations

This is the area the letter treats as unresolved, and it is the area where the public record is strongest.

### The financing trail is fully documented

The relevant document is the **Alaska Municipal Bond Bank Authority board packet for the March 21, 2019 meeting**, which contains the Borough's loan application and PFM Financial Advisors' credit review.

**The loan:**

- Total FNSB request: **$10,745,000**
- Tax-exempt portion: **$6,950,000**
- Taxable portion: **$3,795,000**
- Term: **20 years** (so maturing approximately 2039)
- Security: full faith and credit of the Borough, pledge of property taxes, plus AMBBA state-aid intercept
- **Loan subject to State debt service reimbursement: No**

**The tax-exempt proceeds** funded roof and window improvements at four buildings: Joy Elementary, Ladd Elementary, North Pole High School, and the Juanita Helms Administration Center.

**The taxable proceeds** funded the John Carlson Community Activity Center, Pioneer Park, and the Civic Center.

**The Joy line item, verbatim from the credit review:**

> A portion of the Joy Elementary School roof is leaking. To protect against further leakage and potential interior damage, the district is proposing to replace the roof, insulation and clearstories. In addition to preventing further damage to the building, the improvements will also increase the roof insulation factor from R30 to R100, making the building more energy efficient to operate. The Joy project accounts for **$1.1 million** of total bond proposition.

**Corresponding bond series.** The Borough lists 2019 AMBBA G.O. Series One and Series Two on its own website, including **FNSB Series U at $5,765,000** (tax-exempt) and **taxable Series V at $3,710,000**.

### The finding that matters

**Joy's $1.1 million sits in the tax-exempt portion of the 2019 loan, not the taxable portion.**

This is the single most consequential fact in the financing record, and it explains precisely why bond counsel is involved. Tax-exempt governmental bonds carry private business use restrictions under IRC Section 141. The Carlson Center, Pioneer Park, and Civic Center work was financed on the taxable side, where those restrictions do not bite in the same way. Joy was not.

So the question is narrow and answerable: what portion of the outstanding tax-exempt 2019 bonds is allocable to Joy, and what remedy applies if that property passes to private or nonprofit ownership or use.

### What we could not determine

1. **Current principal outstanding allocable to Joy.** The 2019 packet gives the original $1.1 million allocation, not a present payoff. The Borough's Financial Services Administrative division holds this; its stated role includes municipal bond compliance.
2. **Earliest call date on the relevant maturities.** This governs the remedial action timeline and is in the official statement, which we have located but not yet reconciled maturity by maturity.
3. **Any earlier bond authorizations touching Joy.** The Borough's Index of Propositions references Ordinance No. 2000-012, a $57,000,000 bond covering Two Rivers, Joy, and U-Park Elementary Schools. Whether any of that debt remains outstanding, and whether it was state-reimbursed, needs checking. This is a real open item and we flag it rather than assume it away.
4. **State grant conditions or DEED agreements** attaching to the building separate from bond debt.

**Note on item 3.** The 2019 loan was explicitly not state-reimbursed. Older school construction debt often was. State debt service reimbursement can carry its own conditions on disposition of the reimbursed facility. This is a distinct question from the federal tax question and should not be collapsed into it.

## 3. Options evaluated

The letter asks which structures have been examined. Here is the structural map, drawn from Borough Code and federal regulation.

### Borough Code pathways

**Sale.** Chapter 20.20 FNSBC governs land sale and provides these mechanisms: auction sale (20.20.060), lottery sale (20.20.070), over-the-counter sale (20.20.080), direct sale (20.20.090), exchange sale (20.20.100), cooperative sale (20.20.110), and combination sale (20.20.120).

**Direct sale is not available here.** FNSBC 20.20.090 limits direct sale to those holding a land interest in, or recorded access easement on, the Borough land, or those holding a land interest in adjoining land, and only where there is no public use for the land. It further requires that a mineral or lease interest have been held four years, caps parcels at 40 acres, and requires a sale price no less than 10 percent above fair market value.

This means a negotiated sale directly to North 50, or to any unrelated party, is not a code pathway absent an amendment. The realistic sale routes are competitive.

**Lease.** FNSBC 20.16.020 governs leases of Borough land.

- **20.16.020(B)** requires an Assembly ordinance authorizing any lease.
- **20.16.020(C)** requires a competitive process for land suitable for commercial or industrial use if more than one person in the notification area requests to lease it. Competitive process follows FNSBC Title 16.
- **20.16.020(D)** permits the Assembly to authorize a lease **below fair rental value** only if the authorizing ordinance contains a finding of **compelling public purpose or use** and a statement of the supporting facts.
- **20.16.020(E)** ties lease term to amortization of lessee improvements plus not more than 10 years. Leases over 10 years must be recorded.
- **20.16.020(G)** requires the proposed lessee to submit a property improvement plan with a schedule.

**Precedent.** Ordinance No. 2025-15 used this authority to authorize a below-market nonprofit lease supporting community access and facility rehabilitation, and included a waiver of FNSBC 20.16.020(C). That waiver is instructive: it shows the Assembly has a mechanism for setting aside the competitive-process requirement where it chooses to.

**This does not establish that Joy qualifies.** It establishes that the tool exists, has been used recently, and that the findings-and-facts standard in 20.16.020(D) is the standard any such arrangement would have to meet.

### Federal tax pathways

Under **Treas. Reg. Section 1.141-12**, an issuer that takes a deliberate action changing the use or ownership of bond-financed property has three remedial actions available:

1. **Redemption or defeasance of nonqualified bonds.** Where consideration is exclusively cash, disposition proceeds are used to redeem a pro rata portion of the nonqualified bonds at the earliest call date. If not redeemed within 90 days of the deliberate action, a defeasance escrow must be established within 90 days.
2. **Alternative use of disposition proceeds.** Available where the issuer reasonably expects to spend the proceeds within two years and the use does not cause the private business tests to be met.
3. **Alternative use of the facility itself.**

**Anticipatory remedial action** under 1.141-12(d)(3) allows the issuer to declare official intent to redeem or defease bonds that would become nonqualified, and act *before* the deliberate action. The declaration must identify the financed property and describe the contemplated action.

**Why this matters to the timeline.** The redemption and defeasance remedies run on 90-day clocks keyed to the deliberate action. The anticipatory route lets the Borough resolve the bond question in advance rather than discovering the cost after the fact. That is an argument for doing the analysis now, not after a buyer is selected.

### What we could not determine

1. Which specific 2019 maturities would be the nonqualified bonds, and at what dollar cost.
2. Whether the Borough has considered anticipatory remedial action.
3. Whether the Borough has evaluated retained ownership with nonprofit operation, which changes the private business use analysis materially compared to outright sale.
4. Whether any legal analysis of the FNSBC 20.20 pathway limitation described above has been done.

## 4. Valuation and ownership costs

### Carrying cost, from the Borough's own public figures

- Mayor Hopkins, February 2026: approximately **$140,000 annually**, roughly **$11,500 per month**, for heating and basic upkeep of the vacant building.
- Mayor Hopkins, August 2026: annual utilities budget of approximately **$140,000** to keep Joy operational while unused.

### Accrual since closure

Closure effective July 1, 2025. As of September 12, 2026, that is approximately **14.5 months**.

At the Borough's own stated rate of $11,500 per month:

**Approximately $165,000 to $170,000 in utilities and basic upkeep alone since the building went dark.**

This figure is deliberately conservative. It excludes insurance, security, snow removal, staff time, deferred maintenance accrual, and the opportunity cost of the asset being unavailable for any productive use.

### The cost-saving context

The News-Miner editorial board noted in June 2025 that closing Joy was estimated to save about **$54,000 per year**, while the building would continue to cost money to keep closed. On the Borough's own later numbers, the carrying cost of the closed building is roughly two and a half times the annual saving the closure produced.

The editorial also noted that a local charter school had been actively considering leasing half the building, which would have produced revenue.

### What we could not determine

1. **Current assessed value.** The Borough's Assessing Department holds this. It is a matter of public record and is obtainable, but it is not the same as an appraisal.
2. **Actual spend since closure**, as opposed to the budgeted figure. Only the Borough has this.
3. **Appraisal status.** Ordered when, by whom, due when.
4. **Deferred maintenance backlog** on the building.

**A note on what an appraisal can and cannot do here.** A conventional appraisal establishes fair market value. It does not establish the Borough's net position, because the net position is fair market value minus whatever the bond remedy costs, minus disposition expense. A valuation without the bond analysis is half an answer, and the bond analysis does not require the appraisal to begin.

### What does not require the Borough

Everything in sections 1 through 4 of this document was assembled from public sources in a single working session.

That includes the full financing trail, the exact Joy allocation, the tax-exempt versus taxable split, the code pathways and their limits, the below-market lease precedent and its waiver, the federal remedial action framework and its deadlines, and the carrying cost accrual.

None of it required privileged access, a records request, or Borough staff time.
